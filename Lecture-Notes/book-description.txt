Theoretical Computer Science: Logic & Puzzles is an introduction to mathematical logic for computer scientists. It explains the theory with precise definitions and complete proofs, and then turns every central idea into a working TypeScript program. You will not only learn what a satisfiable formula or a formal proof is. You will write the programs that find satisfying valuations, compute normal forms, unify terms, and search for proofs. Then you will use them to solve puzzles that are hard to solve by hand.

Why should a programmer care about logic? Software failures have canceled more than 16,700 flights at Southwest Airlines, brought down two Boeing 737 MAX airliners, and sent innocent employees of the British Post Office to prison. Building systems that work requires precision, and logic provides it. Logic is also the basis of SAT solvers, of constraint solvers such as Microsoft's Z3, and of the tools that verify digital circuits. The author worked on such tools at the Siemens Research Center until 2002.

What you will learn
- Limits of computability: why no program can decide whether another program terminates, and why the equivalence of programs is undecidable.
- Correctness proofs: computational induction for recursive programs, and symbolic execution with loop invariants for iterative ones.
- Propositional logic: syntax, semantics, normal forms, the cut rule and its completeness, and the Davis-Putnam algorithm with the Jeroslow-Wang heuristic.
- First-order logic: syntax and semantics, constraint programming, a backtracking constraint solver, symbolic transition systems, and the Z3 solver.
- Automated theorem proving: clausal form, Skolemization, unification, resolution, and the provers Vampire, Prover9, and Mace4.

Logic & puzzles
Puzzles are the book's laboratory. You will place eight queens on a chessboard, solve the Zebra Puzzle and a Sudoku, color the map of Australia, crack SEND + MORE = MONEY, ferry missionaries and cannibals across a river, lead a knight across all 64 squares, and let Vampire find out who killed Aunt Agatha. You first build a simple solver yourself, so that you understand how it works. Then you use an industrial tool such as MiniSat or Z3 to see how far the same ideas reach.

Theory you can run
All listings are printed in full, with line numbers, and explained line by line. Every program is also available as a Jupyter notebook at github.com/karlstroetmann/Logic, ready to run and modify.

Who this book is for
The book grew out of the author's logic course and is written for computer science students, typically in their first year. It also suits programmers who want to know what SAT solvers and theorem provers do, developers who want to reason about the correctness of their code, and instructors who need a course text with ready-to-use programs. You should be comfortable reading programs and know basic set notation and proof by induction. No previous knowledge of logic is required.

Features
- 188 pages in US Letter format, so that the listings are printed at full width
- Full-color interior with syntax-highlighted code
- Exercises throughout, many with a prepared notebook, and review questions at the end of every chapter
- A bibliography, a list of acronyms, and a detailed index

Logic is the part of computer science that does not go out of date. This book teaches it with proofs that explain and programs that run.
